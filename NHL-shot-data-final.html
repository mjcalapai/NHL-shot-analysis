What these types of shots look like (short two minute video): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=THauVgajPiM

  1. Description of the metadata

Each row corresponds to one shot attempt take in the NHL. This includes shots which become goals, saves, or shots which miss the net/hit the post. The only shot attempt type not included are blocked shots (blocked by a skater). This extensive dataset has 124 parameters. These include the shotID, home and away teams, when the shot happened (down to the second of the game), the result of the game, where the shot happened (including the city, and coordiante one the ice), the angle of the shot, and many parameters regarding situations happening in the game at that moment. Examples of these situations are the amount of players on the ice, how long they have been on the ice for, whether they are playing on their expected side of the ice or not, and details regarding the events immediately before and after the shot. Finally, there are are parameters which may be relevant regarding statistics, such as expected goals, expected rebound, expected play stopped on shot, etc. This represents the expected value of a shot becoming a goal. For example, this will normall be higher in the middle of the ice, close to the net.

  1. Rationale

Hockey is notoriously difficult if not impossible to predict using data analysis. This is the most comprehensive dataset I could find regarding it. I understand what each of the 124 parameters represent, and how to use them in conjunction with each other properly to derive conclusions. There could not be a dataset which I feel as if I am more of an expert in, regardless of the size. However, I do notice a problem with this dataset. It does not include anthing regarding the goaltender position, which is the most influential position to the outcome of a hockey game, and the highly influential for shot data for obvious reasons.

  • How has shooting changed over the past 19 years?
  • Where should a player shoot from? What type of shot should a player take in certain areas of the ice?

shooter_stats <- df %>%
  filter(
    !is.na(shooterName),
    !is.na(goal),
    !is.na(xGoal)
  ) %>%
  group_by(shooterName) %>%
  summarise(
    shots = n(),
    goals = sum(goal, na.rm = TRUE),
    expected_goals = sum(xGoal, na.rm = TRUE),

    shooting_pct = goals / shots,

    goals_above_expected = goals - expected_goals,

    # Finishing ability normalized for volume of shots
    goals_above_expected_per_100 =
      100 * goals_above_expected / shots,
    # Variance of sum of Bernoulli trials
    expected_variance = sum(xGoal * (1 - xGoal)),
    expected_sd = sqrt(expected_variance),
    #How unusual the player's actual goal total is compared with the xG model
    z_score = goals_above_expected / expected_sd,
    .groups = "drop"
  )

#min sample size for a player to be considered in the analysis, 19 year dataset
qualified_shooters <- shooter_stats %>%
  filter(shots >= 500)

These results match my personal hypothesis regarding slapshots and one-timers. Although the one-timer is a proxy, I believe that it is a lot more common to see this type of shot in the modern NHL due to the high skill level of players today. However, this rate of increase is very dramatic and may not be completely true. One timers are most common on the powerplay, when the other team has a penalty against them. Taking additional data regarding the amount of penalties called in the modern NHL could be helpful to derive insights from, as the referees are regarded as more strict now than the 2000's era and before.

Another piece of note worth information is the dips in goals in 2013 and 2020. This is because less games were played in those particular years. In 2013 there was a lockout from the NHLPA (National Hockey League Players Association) regarding union disputes for pay. In 2020 there were less games played due to COVID-19.

  1. Limitations

The biggest limitation comes from the lack of data regarding context of events. For example, passes. Passing will greatly affect situations. Where the pass was initiated from, how fast it traveled, time since the pass was executed before the shot would be very useful information. For example it would allow me to figure out the rate of how one-timers have been used in the NHL with the rising skill level of the league. Another limitation is the lack of definition of what a slapshot is. Based on the data, it seems to have a very loose definition. I interpret it being defined in the data as "the stick is in the air, hits the ice before hitting the puck, and one touches the puck on the shot". However I would not define this as a slapshot personally. At the same time, it may not be defined as that based on the most common shots per area. To summarize, the lack of context is the biggest limitation of this dataset, and it is the limitation to applying data to hockey in general.
